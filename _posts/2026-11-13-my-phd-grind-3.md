Ask PhD students what matters most in a PhD and you'll hear all kinds of answers: the school, the topic, the advisor's character... But almost every answer has one thing in common: the advisor.

Before my PhD — three years of research as an undergrad, plus a gap year after that — I had worked with more than a dozen professors. I met every type and learned plenty of lessons the hard way; I'd seen nearly every kind of advisor, character-wise, that people run into when choosing a PhD advisor. There was the kind who knew nothing and was short-sighted, yet was an expert at exploiting people with every trick in the book. There was the kind who wanted me to target ICLR, three months away, when I had barely started on a topic. There was the kind who deliberately cut my pay. And there was also the kind whose goal was to train students, who looked out for their students at every turn, and who gave me plenty of freedom and respect. So, having seen what a good advisor looks like before my PhD, I wasn't going to lower my standards on an advisor's character for anything else (like the research topic). In fact, in every collaboration since, I have never again lowered my standards on "character" for anything. In my three years as a PhD student, very few people or situations have required me to swallow my pride and go against my own nature. I've crossed paths with the wrong people now and then, but I've almost never met anyone truly bad — anyone who left me with real psychological scars. (For reference, look up the Keyu Tian "poisoning" incident.) We shouldn't view people in academia through rose-tinted glasses. If you start a PhD wearing them, you may go through a very painful stretch of taking them off.

When it comes to advisors, Cornell CS has a pretty good culture. I rarely heard of faculty with a terrible reputation, or ones who felt awful to work with — so much so that I seemed to have almost too many options to try. So for the first year and a half of my PhD, I bounced back and forth between three advisors: all three of them were great, and I was in an exploring phase, so I tried a lot of things.

Throughout my first year, I never quite figured out what I wanted, so I was doing theory with Sarah on one side and NLP with Claire, my current advisor, on the other.

Sarah was the first person who really taught me how to write a paper.

I'd had a few papers before my PhD, but no one had ever taught me the way she did. Back then I barely used GPT, so every sentence in my papers — including the clumsy, convoluted ones — was typed out by me, word by word.

Sarah would leave lots and lots of comments: what she thought the problem was, how she thought it should be changed, and a more detailed revision I could use as a reference. This part is really my personal preference. If she had only listed a pile of issues without telling me how to fix them, I might not have known what to change; if she had just rewritten things herself — even though that would have saved her a lot of time — I might not have understood why she chose to change them that way. Except for the appendix, she read every corner of the paper carefully, from start to finish, and revised it many times over.

That's also one of the reasons I didn't want to leave the theory community back then: your collaborators and advisors don't just put their names on your paper — they genuinely polish the work with you, even if no one outside a handful of people in your subfield will ever care about it. They're willing to spend time training a student. The days I spent revising papers with Sarah are among the few happy memories of my PhD. Later, during rebuttals, we discussed every single one together: I'd write a draft, and she'd comment and revise. We did have small conflicts now and then, but in the days before a deadline, writing the paper together, I'd once again feel that Sarah was the best advisor in the world.

In the beginning, I honestly didn't know how to work with Claire. I didn't yet have the ability to do research on my own. What I needed was an advisor who would guide me step by step. I was also extremely insecure about my research — I often felt that what I was doing was garbage — and I needed to draw energy from someone else (my advisor, for example). I needed my advisor to care about my research more than I did. (My advisor before my PhD, Preslav, was exactly that kind of person.) On top of that, I still leaned toward continuing with theory, but the kind of theory Sarah did was quite different from what I'd worked on as an undergrad; I wanted to do theory closer to RL/ML. I was trying hard to find a direction of moderate difficulty (i.e., one where I could actually follow the proofs), and anyone who did theory back in my day might agree: RL theory was a pretty easy place to crank out papers. So I went to Wen. He was already gradually moving from RL theory to LLMs, and he told me plainly that he was no longer interested in theory. I can't remember what I did after that, but somehow it ended with him and Sarah co-advising me on an RL theory project, and I formally said goodbye to Claire.

Wen agreed to co-advise on one condition: we would all meet together and work on a joint project. But — maybe I was being too sensitive — once I'd forced the two of them together, the vibe in our meetings felt a little strange. They both seemed to have plenty of ideas and things they could do. If they hadn't cared about each other's feelings, each could have steered the project toward their own research direction, but out of respect for each other, neither did. Eventually, the project was bound to lean toward one person's interests, leaving the other without any real sense of ownership. I had never been co-advised before, let alone in an arrangement I had forced together myself. I quickly realized the problem, so, awkwardly, I went back to Claire and asked whether she would take me back in — co-advising me with Wen. (Yes: less than two weeks later, I went crawling back to Claire with my tail between my legs.)

And so began the Wen-and-Claire co-advising chapter. Later on, because of some things that happened, my mental state became extremely bad and I wanted to drop out. Claire supported every decision I made. During that period I probably came to almost every meeting with a "new decision" — that I wanted to quit, and later, that I wanted to try to graduate in three years — and every time I brought her one of these "surprises," she never once brushed it off as something I'd said in an unstable moment. Claire walked with me through the hardest stretch of my second year. Later still, my research skills improved, and I gradually became able to do research independently. I no longer needed a hands-on advisor to feed me ideas or tell me what to do at every step. At that point, having Claire as my sole advisor seemed to fit better than co-advising (there were other reasons too, of course), so I told Wen I no longer needed to be co-advised, and Wen agreed. A year later, in that great wave of assistant professors leaving academia, Wen left for industry. Half a year after that, he suddenly came to mind one day, so I set up a meeting with him just to catch up — and heard Wen speak Chinese for the first time. Somehow his English seemed better than his Chinese. He seemed very happy in industry.

Over these years, I've heard plenty of horror stories from other schools: departments with several faculty members notorious for their bad character; departments where the Director of Graduate Studies (DGS), instead of protecting students, goes out of their way to find fault with them; and places where PhD students compete fiercely with one another to "grab" advisors.

But Cornell CS does well on all of these fronts. Most of the faculty are good people and very supportive, and so is the DGS.

More than half of the people in our department have switched advisors, or gone through a period without one. Whenever that happens, everyone goes to the DGS, and the DGS always helps figure something out. As someone independent of the advisors, the DGS always takes the student's side and gives students as much support as possible.

So in my two years at Cornell, I hardly heard of anyone quitting. In other departments, though, and at other schools of the same caliber, I've heard of plenty of students being driven out by their department or advisor over these years.

Admittedly, not many people in the department have tried this many advisor combinations in such a short time, and I'm very grateful to my advisors for their patience and understanding. My story with Claire deserves a post of its own. I actually met her by chance at the end of 2022, at EMNLP, on the bus to the conference banquet. I wasn't doing NLP yet and didn't know who she was; she just looked familiar, so I started chatting with her. Luckily, Claire was fairly low-key: she wasn't surrounded by a crowd, and apart from her former students, almost no one stopped her to talk. As an outsider who had finally found someone to talk to, I stuck with her the whole way, until she left the banquet.

Back then, I had no idea that two months later I'd get an offer from Cornell — or that this woman with short blonde hair and a kind face, as calm as a summer sky and as warm as spring sunshine, would end up becoming my PhD advisor, walking with me through the whole long, painful PhD grind and watching me grow into who I am now.

I know that many advisors are nothing like mine (Claire, Sarah, and Wen). Some are hypocrites. Some take putting other people down as their only joy in life. Some will do anything to get what they want. Some show one face to their peers and to people more senior than them, and quite another to their students...

Even with a great advisor, a PhD hurts in all sorts of ways. Many people reach a point where they want to quit, and then, because of certain people or certain things, they hang on and see it through to the end. I think writing these things down might help some people see what a good advisor looks like, so that they stop treating some advisors' behavior as normal. As independent individuals, it isn't our place to change how other people behave, but at the very least, we can choose who we spend our time with.

如果你问那些博士生们读博最重要的是什么，或许你能得到很多答案：学校、topic、导师的人品……但几乎每个人的答案里，都会包含“导师”。

在读博之前，本科做科研的三年以及后面的gap的一年，我也前前后后接触过十多个professor了，各种类型的人都见过，踩了很多坑，几乎把大家读博选导师会遇到的不同人品的导师类型都见了一遍，有什么都不懂，目光短浅，但擅长使用各种手段压榨的，有要求我在一个topic刚开始做的时候就target 3个月之后的ICLR的，有故意削减工资的，也有以培养学生为目标，处处为学生着想，给我足够的自由和尊重的。所以在读博之前，因为见过了好的导师应该是什么样的，我不会因为其他的因素（比如研究课题等）而降低我对导师人品的标准。事实上，在我后面的任何合作中，我都没有再因为任何东西降低我对“人品”的要求，读博这三年，很少有人和事需要让我去委曲求全，违背自己的天性。虽然有时候也会遇人不淑，但真正遇到坏人、给我造成精神创伤的情况几乎没有。此处大家可以refer to “田柯宇投毒事件”，大家不应该对学术界的人带有滤镜，如果你是带着滤镜来读博，那你可能会经历一段十分痛苦的摘掉滤镜的时光。

在导师方面，Cornell CS算是氛围比较好的，我很少听到那些reputation特别差的，或者接触起来感觉特别差的老师，以至于我可以尝试的选择似乎有点过多了，于是，我读博的前一年半都在三个导师之间反复横跳，因为他们三个都很棒，而且我处在一个explore的阶段，所以进行了很多尝试。

我读博第一年里一直没有想清楚自己想要什么，于是一边在跟Sarah做理论，另一边在和我现在的导师Claire做NLP。

Sarah是第一个真正教我怎么写论文的人。

读博之前，我虽然也有过一些文章，但从来没有人像她这样教过我。那时候我还没怎么使用GPT，文章里的每一个句子，包括那些蹩脚的、绕来绕去的句子，都是我自己一个字一个字敲出来的。

Sarah会在上面留很多很多comment，包括她觉得这里会有什么问题，她觉得应该怎么改，以及一个更详细的可以参考的改法。其实这里是我个人的preference，如果她只是提一堆意见，而不告诉我怎么改，我可能会不知道怎么改动，如果她直接上手改，我则可能不知道她为什么要选择那样改，即使这会节省她很多时间。除了附录，文章的每一个角落，她都会从头到尾、认真地读、改很多遍。

这也是我那时候不愿意离开理论这个community的原因之一：你的合作者和导师不只是在你的文章上挂个名字，而是真正和你一起在打磨这个作品，即使这个作品除了学术圈同领域的几个人外没有人会关心。他们愿意花时间去培养一个学生。和Sarah一起改论文的日子，是读博期间为数不多的快乐的记忆了。后面rebuttal的时候，每个rebuttal也是和她一起讨论，我写出草稿，然后她来评论和修改。虽然平时我也偶尔会和Sarah有些小矛盾，但到了deadline前写论文的那几天，我又会觉得，Sarah是全天下最好的导师。

最开始，我其实不太知道该怎么和Claire相处。那时候的我，还没有独立做research的能力。我需要的是一个能够手把手带我的导师，而且当时的我对我的research极其不自信，常常觉得自己做的东西是垃圾，需要从别人（比如我的导师）那里吸取能量，我需要我的导师比我更关心我的research。（比如，我读博之前的导师Preslav就是那样的人。）另外，我当时还是更偏向于继续做理论一些，但Sarah做的理论又和我之前本科做的东西不太一样，我还是想做偏RL/ML的理论。我当时在努力找一个难度适中（i.e., 我能读懂证明）的方向，在我那个时代做过理论的人或许会认同：RL theory还是很好水paper的。于是我去找了Wen，他当时已经逐渐在从RL theory转向LLM，明确告诉我他对theory已经不感兴趣，但我忘了我当时干了些啥，反正最后就是决定了他和Sarah一起co-advise做一个RL相关的理论的project，我也正式地和Claire说了再见。

Wen同意co-advise的前提是大家一起meet，做一个joint project，但不知道是不是我太sensitive了，他俩被我强行凑到一起后，meet的vibe有点奇怪，感觉他们都有很多想法以及可以做的东西，如果他们不考虑对方的感受，便可以把project往他们各自的研究方向上引，但又出于对对方的尊重，没有那么做。后面结果必然是这个project会偏向他俩中的某一个人的interest，剩下一个人则会没有参与感。我之前从来没有被co-advise过，更别提这样由我自己强行凑出来的co-advise了。我很快地意识到了问题，于是，我尴尬地回去找了Claire，问她能不能继续收留我，和Wen一起co-advise。（没错，才过了不到两周我就灰溜溜地回去找Claire了。）

于是又开启了Wen和Claire的co-advise，再到后面，因为一些事情，我的精神状态变得极差，想要退学，Claire支持我做的每一个决定。虽然我可能在那段时间，每次meeting都会告诉她一个“新的决定”，比如，告诉她我想要quit，以及后面告诉她我想尝试三年毕业，我每次给她带来这些“surprise”，但她都没有觉得那些是我在情绪不稳定时说的玩笑话。Claire陪我走过了读博第二年那段最难的时光。再后面，我的research能力有了一些长进，我逐渐能够独立地做科研了，我不再需要一个hands-on的老师来喂给我一个idea，或者指导我每一步应该做什么了。这时，Claire单独作为我的advisor似乎比co-advise更合适了（当然，还有一些其他的原因），我跟Wen提出我不再需要co-advise了。Wen便答应了。一年后，Wen在那波浩浩荡荡的AP离职热潮中，离开了学术界，加入了工业界。又过了半年，我某天突然想起他来，便和Wen约了个meeting闲聊，第一次听到了Wen讲中文，怎么感觉Wen的英文比他的中文讲得好，他在工业界似乎过得很开心。

读博这几年，我听过很多其他学校的horror story：有的系里，有好几个出了名人品有问题的老师；有的学院DGS不但不保护学生，反而处处给学生找茬儿；还有的地方，PhD之间因为要“抢”导师，大家竞争激烈。

但Cornell的CS，在这些方面都做得很好。大部分老师人品都很好，也都很supportive，DGS也是。

我们系超过半数的人都换过导师，或者有过一段找不到导师的时间。每到这种时候，大家都会去找DGS，而DGS总会帮大家想办法，作为一个和导师independent的存在，DGS总是站在学生的角度，给学生最大的支持。

所以，在Cornell的两年里，我几乎没有听说过谁quit。倒是其他学院以及其他同等level的学校，这几年里，我听到过很多学生被学院或者导师逼走的case。

系里像我这样在短时间内尝试过这么多排列组合的确实不多，我也非常感谢我的导师们的包容和理解。和Claire的事情，我觉得值得单独写一篇了。我其实是在22年底在EMNLP上去晚宴的大巴上偶遇的Claire，那时的我还没有开始做NLP，也并不认识Claire，觉得她面熟，便跟她聊了起来。还好Claire比较低调，她没有被一堆人包围，除了她之前的学生外，几乎没有人把她拦住讲话，作为局外人的我好不容易找到了个可以跟我讲话的人，便跟了她一路，直到她离开晚宴。

当时的我不会想到自己两个月后会收到Cornell的offer，也想不到那个金色短发、面容和蔼、像夏日的天空一样平静、像春天的阳光一样温暖的女士，最后会成为我的PhD导师，陪我完整地走完这长长、痛苦的PhD grind，看着我成长为现在的样子。

我知道，很多导师都不是我的导师们（Claire、Sarah、Wen）这样的：有的人虚伪；有的人以打压别人作为生活中唯一的快乐；有的人可以为了他们想要的东西不择手段；有的人对自己的同辈以及更senior的人一套嘴脸而对学生又是另一套……

即使导师很好，也会有各种各样的读博的痛苦之处。很多人在读博的某个阶段都有想要quit的时刻，又因为一些人或是一些事坚持了下来，最后完整地读了下来。我想，把这些事情写下来，或许能帮助一些人看到，好的导师是什么样的，或许就不会觉得一些导师的行为是理所应当的了。作为独立的个体，我们没有理由去改变他人的行为，但是我们至少可以选择和什么样的人相处。
